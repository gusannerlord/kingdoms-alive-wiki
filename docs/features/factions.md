# Factions and grievances

Lords do not only plot against the throne. They remember what the crown has done to them, and they band together behind a cause: a charter to bind the ruler's hands, the fall of a house that has grown too great, or a new ruler. A faction tries the council first, the lawful way. Only when the council fails it does it put its case to the ruler, and only when the ruler refuses does it think of arms.

All of this happens in every kingdom, not only yours. You can join a faction, lead one, betray one to the crown, or face them from the throne.

## Lords remember

Every house keeps a short list of wrongs, each with a name, a place and a date. Lords speak of them in conversation, and the worst of them show in the [kingdom screen's Factions tab](civil-war.md#the-factions-tab).

| What happened | Against whom |
|---|---|
| A town or castle of theirs fell to a siege while an army of the realm, led by another house, lingered within a day's ride for half a day or more, free to come, and never fought for it | the house that led that army (the crown, if it was the ruler's army or his family's) |
| A town or castle of theirs fell to a siege, no army of the realm marched to it or lingered near it, and no lord of another house fought for it or stood within its walls | the crown, and the house of the realm's [Marshal](royal-court.md#the-marshals-burden) |
| A conquered fief they stood for was given to another house (twice running weighs double) | the crown, and a little the house that got it |
| The ruler's own house took a conquered fief they stood for | the crown |
| The council voted to take a fief from them | the crown, and whoever proposed it |
| The ruler gave a fief of theirs to their rivals by decree, in a feud | the crown |
| The realm bought its peace with tribute | the crown |
| A kinsman was executed | the crown, or the house that did it |
| The crown shielded a house they wanted brought down, or spurned their charter | the crown |
| The crown took a conquered fief for itself while a charter forbade it | the crown |
| The ruler's house holds **6 or more** towns and castles, more than **15%** of the realm's | the crown |
| A vassal house holds **4 or more** towns and castles, more than **10%** of the realm's | that house (not the crown) |
| They came to your court to ask for a fief of yours they claim by right, and you refused | the crown |

**Too much land in one hand.** Every 30 days, while the ruler's house holds more than five towns and castles and more than 15% of the realm's, every other house (not the ruler's kin) holds it a little more against the crown: 2 for each fief beyond the fifth. A vassal house with more than three that are more than 10% of the realm's is begrudged the same way, against that house alone (its own kin excepted). It is one grudge per house that keeps growing while the hoard lasts, and fades like any other once it ends; it costs goodwill only while it is still rising, so a hoard that lasts costs the ruler (or that house) a fair amount of goodwill over the first months and nothing more after that, though the grudge stays. It counts the same for you, as ruler or vassal, and a hoarding house is the natural target of a [reckoning](#the-reckoning-of-a-house).

**Grudges between houses.** When one house of an AI realm comes to hold a wrong against another, their heads fall out at once: a house passed over in a share-out thinks less of the house that got the fief; a house that left the realm alone with a foe at the walls during a feud loses the goodwill of every other house, and much more if it defies the crown to the end or goes over to the foe. The same holds against your house: a house you beat to a share-out, or leave alone against a foe in a feud, thinks less of you; and if your house defies the crown to the end, or goes over to the foe, in a feud, every house of the realm turns against you at once, as it would against any other. Your own feelings are never moved for you: whom you like is up to you.

**Who came, and who fought.** While a fief is besieged, the realm watches which armies come near it, and whether they really helped: one of their parties fought at that siege (breaking the besiegers from outside, sallying out with the garrison, holding the walls against the assault) or rode into the fief. An army that lingered close and did nothing is what the house remembers when the fief falls, and its head thinks less of whoever led it, you included. Only the hours it was free to come count: not while it was besieging another fief, fighting a battle, relieving or holding another besieged fief of the realm, still gathering its lords, or already marching to this one or chasing its besiegers. An army still on its way when the walls fall is not blamed. If the house had asked you in person to relieve the fief (see below) and you answered, your answer and what you did count instead. A lone lord riding past no longer spares the crown; only a lord who fought for the fief does. A siege that is lifted leaves no grudge.

**A plea from your host.** When you lead an army and a fief of a house riding in it is besieged not far off, one of its lords (the head of the house, if they ride with you) rides up beside you in their war gear and, as one who marches under your banner, asks you to lead the army to its relief: once per siege and house, however many of them ride with you. Refuse and the house thinks less of you. Promise and it watches: if the fief falls and your host never fought for it nor rode into it, your word is remembered as broken, and that costs more than refusing; if you did help, the house is grateful when the siege ends, whether the fief held or not. If the siege is lifted without you, nobody minds. Help after refusing is remembered kindly too.

**Grudges against the crown.** A wrong a house lays at its ruler's door (a fief lost with no help sent, passed over, the king's share, a shameful peace, a ruler who shields their enemy or spurns them, a broken charter, a fief given away by the ruler's decree) also costs a little of its head's goodwill toward the ruler, whether the ruler is of the AI or you. A little each time: a ruler who fails the same house again and again makes an enemy of it, and an enemy of the ruler is who starts a plot. A good [Chancellor](royal-court.md#the-offices) softens it: the more charming, the less goodwill the wrongs cost, down to half only with a truly exceptional one (a small wrong may cost none at all), though the house remembers the wrong just the same. A fief taken by the council's vote or an executed kinsman costs that goodwill the way the game already does, not twice. Your goodwill toward a ruler, as a vassal, is never moved for you.

**Great realms breed rancour.** In a kingdom grown beyond its fair share of Calradia, every wrong against the crown weighs more when it is born, up to twice as much in a kingdom of twice its fair share, and costs the ruler that much more goodwill. The lords of such a kingdom also band together more readily: they lean further to every faction, and a smaller grudge is enough to begin a Reckoning (see [Great realms, restless lords](civil-war.md#great-realms-restless-lords)). Grudges between houses do not grow with the realm.

Wrongs fade with time (an executed kinsman, much more slowly). When a new house takes the throne, the old grievances against the crown weigh only half.

A house's grievances make it readier to join a [plot against the throne](civil-war.md) and any faction against the crown. So does serving a ruler of another [faith](faiths.md), and a ruler who refused to step down when the lords asked.

## The Charter

Houses with grievances against the crown write a charter of up to three clauses:

- to strike down the crown's strongest laws in force (such as Royal Privilege, Sacred Majesty or War Tax);
- to make law one of the lords' own (such as the Senate or the Lords' Privy Council);
- **fiefs to the realm**: if the ruler has been keeping conquests for his own house, that for a while the ruler's house takes no share of conquered fiefs.

The faction brings its laws before the council one by one, and all its lords vote together. If the council turns it down, or a clause cannot be voted, it puts the whole charter to the ruler and waits for an answer.

The ruler may **sign it** (the laws change and each lord forgets the heaviest of his grievances), **buy its leader** (he goes quiet for a good while, and the faction looks for another head), **strike at the leader** (take his office or put one of his fiefs to the council), or **refuse**. Striking does not end the ultimatum. When the time runs out without a yes, the charter rises if half the realm stands behind it, the realm is at peace with every kingdom and no other AI civil war holds it back; if not, it asks once more after a while, and the second time it breaks up bitter. A charter does not wait for the peace: in a war its time runs out all the same, and it cannot rise. AI rulers seldom sign: charters get their laws mostly by winning the council's votes. A charter's rising is a [civil war](civil-war.md) in which the ruler keeps the throne if he loses, and signs.

## The Reckoning of a house

Lords band together to take a fief from another house of the realm, but only a fief one of them has a [right](fief-rights.md) to. A grudge against that house, or its having grown too great (a quarter of the realm's strength or more), is what moves them; a firm right is reason enough on its own for a house that has no love for the holder. The ruler's own fiefs are never the target.

They propose taking it in the council and vote together. If the council keeps the fief with the house, they ask the ruler. A ruler who gives way puts it to the council himself, and its lords watch how he votes. A ruler who shields the house is remembered for it.

**When you start one**, you choose. Tell a lord *"There is a fief in this realm held against a better right than its holder's."*, then pick the house and the town or castle. Only fiefs to which your house or the lord's has a right are offered, each with whose right it is and how firm. Then choose who should have it: your house (if it has the right), the lord's (if theirs does), or whoever the council chooses. How likely the lord is to agree is said in words, and depends on their grudge against that house, their bonds with it and with you, and whether the fief is promised to them. As you win other lords over you can promise the fief to them too (*"Stand with us... and it will be yours"*), which draws them in. When the fief is taken from the house, the house it was promised to stands among the candidates of the share-out, and the whole reckoning votes for it. The ruler still has the last word. AI reckonings go after the fief their leader has the firmest right to, for their leader. A reckoning whose houses no longer have a right to anything the house holds comes to an end. An AI house presses a claim [by marriage](fief-rights.md#a-claim-by-marriage) only when the two heads are on bad terms; you may press yours at any time. A house never turns on its own blood, and on its in-laws only over a fief it claims through that marriage.

**What the stripped house does.** A house that loses a fief to a reckoning's vote has three ways to take it, and its head's character decides:

- **It breaks with the realm**, taking its lands with it, just as when the ruler [takes a fief from a house](royal-court.md#taking-a-fief-from-a-vassal). No house gives up its land lightly: the brave and proud, and those who hate the ruler, are the likeliest to dare it; the honourable and the calculating, the least. A house that breaks away cannot buy its way back to peace on a whim (see [Peace needs both sides](war-and-peace.md#peace-needs-both-sides)).
- **It holds the fief by force.** A house that will not break with the realm may still bar its gates against the council's verdict. Then the reckoning takes up arms against it in a feud (see below), and the ruler lets the swords settle it: the council has already spoken. A brave head is likelier to do this; an honourable one bows to the council, and a calculating one or a friend of the ruler thinks twice. If no feud can be had (another one already divides the realm, or the realm is at war), the house yields instead.
- **It yields**, and the fief is shared out.

If it is your house, you are asked first: bend the knee, or break your oath.

**Quarrels wait for the peace.** While the realm is at war with another kingdom, reckonings still form and gather lords, but they bring nothing to a head: an AI reckoning puts no vote before the council, none puts its case to the ruler (the days of a demand already made stand still), and no feud or break-away begins. A house stripped of a fief by a vote in wartime yields. No defeat is counted for the wait, and they wait for as long as the war lasts. Wars with houses without a kingdom, minor factions or bandits do not count. If you lead a reckoning, you can still bring its vote before the council yourself. While the war lasts, no vassal (you included) may put a fief before the council either; only the ruler may still take one into the crown's hands. When a reckoning or a feud your house is part of has to wait, its head tells you so, and you hear again when the war is over.

## When a reckoning takes up arms: feuds between houses

A reckoning that the ruler refused may not leave it there. When its last demand runs out, its houses may take up arms against the house they would bring down, to take the fief by force. An AI reckoning the council turns down may do the same, unwilling to abide its word. A reckoning that won its vote also takes up arms when the house [holds the fief by force](#the-reckoning-of-a-house) against the council; the sides are drawn the same way, but the ruler is not asked and lets them fight it out, and the feud waits for the peace if the realm is at war. If such a feud never begins, the council's verdict stands and the fief goes to the share-out. An AI reckoning does so now and then, more readily under a bold or stronger leader, less under an honourable one. But if the claimants give up while still gathering their men, before they ever send their defiance, or their leading house is no longer there to take it, or you lead the reckoning and let it go, the house that barred its gates keeps the fief: nobody came for it, and the council's vote comes to nothing. A realm can have two feuds at a time, but no house rides in two, nor is one fief fought over twice. If you lead the reckoning, you decide: turned down by the council, you are asked whether to take up arms. If a reckoning's vote goes against your own house, you may **bar the gates** and hold the fief by force, as a house of the AI may; the reckoning then comes for it.

**A right pressed by the sword.** A feud needs no reckoning. Now and then a house of the AI with a [right](fief-rights.md) to a fief another house of its realm holds sends that house its defiance and comes to take it, if its side is strong enough, counting the allies that would answer its call, the houses with a cause to join it, and what its purse could hire. You may do the same: speak to the head of the house that holds a fief your house has a right to, and send your defiance; the same houses are out of reach for you (kin, close friends, the ruler's house), but whether your side is strong enough is yours to judge. A firm right, a bold head with little honour, and bad blood with the holder all make it likelier. Kin and friends never come to this, and neither does the ruler's own house. If the fief is yours, you are asked whether to fight or give it up; if the realm is yours, you answer as ruler.

- **Allies called to arms.** Each [ally](#alliances-between-houses) of the head of either side is called first, and most answer; an honourable one, or one who likes the caller well, all the more. One allied to both heads stays out. An ally who refuses breaks the alliance, and the house that called holds it against him. If you are the ally called, you are asked.
- **The two sides.** Each of the reckoning's houses that hold land may ride with its leader, the bolder the likelier, and surely so if it was promised the fief (with a right pressed by the sword, the claimant rides alone at first). The house under attack is joined by its kin and its close friends. Other houses ride only if they are kin or close friends of one side: one bound to both goes with the one it likes best, and a house bound to neither stays home. Now and then an honourable house with no ties to either side, and no enmity with the holder, stands with the holder, when the claimants' [right](fief-rights.md) to the fief is weaker than the holder's; the more honourable, the likelier.
- **Your part in it.** When a feud breaks out in your realm, the head of a house that is your kin or your friend writes to ask your help: the claimants to ride with them, the holder to stand with them. Bound to both, you get both letters and choose one side, or neither. At any time while the feud lasts you may also offer your sword yourself: speak to the head of either house (or, while the head is a prisoner, to a grown lord of the house), or send an envoy with the offer. A head who likes you little turns you down; a calculating one takes help where he finds it. Joining earns you the ill will of the other side's head. If you serve the realm as a mercenary, you offer your company instead: the house pays you once, if its coffers can bear it, you leave the realm's service (an unfinished contract counts as broken), and you ride with its host as a sellsword, sharing its wars (the realm's foes are its foes), until the feud is done, when they let you go. As anywhere, a sellsword is given no fief: a castle your company storms goes to the house that leads the host.
- **The ruler's answer.** The ruler can let them be, forbid the feud, or stand with one side. A ruler of the AI most often lets them be. Forbidding it or taking a side costs a great deal of influence, and only a ruler whose right to rule the lords hold secure can do either. A bold leader may defy a crown that stood against them, and now and then a bold, honourless one defies even its ban, as you may.
- **The fight.** For the length of the feud each side rides out as a host of its own, under the banner of its leading house: a kingdom in all but name, with the realm's laws and customs, that raises armies, lays sieges and rides to the relief of its walls like any other. The houses keep their lands, and their lords are still sworn to the crown. The two hosts are at war only with each other, and with the realm's foes as before. No other kingdom may make war or peace with a host, nor ally with it, and no lord or sellsword of the AI takes service in one. A host holds no council: a fief it takes stays with the house that took it until the feud is settled. When the feud is over, the hosts are broken up.
- **To arms.** When a feud breaks out, both sides call up their men: for some days, lords whose companies are thin make recruiting their first care, riding to the towns and villages that would give them men and paying for them as always; the garrisons are left to hold the walls. Each host also hires free companies of sellswords, the nearest first, as long as its richest house can pay them; they ride with it until the feud is over. Once the feud is declared, the lords of both sides may also hire the [companies for hire](free-companies.md) of the taverns, as any lord at war does, if their purses can bear the whole term, taking turns so that neither side takes them all; nobody hires while a feud is still being prepared in silence. A company so hired serves its whole term, even if the feud ends first. The lords of a host can gather an army with lighter companies than a realm would ask, and march it on the walls. Before the walls of the fief in dispute they need no more strength than the walls hold, where a realm's army would want twice as much, since a lesser house can look for little relief. Nor do they storm it while its walls stand: they let their engines batter them down first, for a week or so at most, and then storm it all the same. While the feud is fought, every lord's company of the houses riding in it holds a fifth more men than usual (yours too); once it is over they are back to their usual size, and the extra men drift away.
- **Gathering before the defiance.** A house of the AI that means to take up arms does not send its defiance at once. Its lords and their friends first gather their men in silence, making recruiting their first care, and declare only once their companies are nearly full and the walls are within their reach. If the men cannot be found in time, the quarrel is let be, and nobody need ever know how close it came.
- **No hopeless feuds.** A house of the AI does not take up arms over a fief its side could never besiege, even with every man called up and every sellsword and company it could afford; a side that comes close may try, counting on the men it will still raise and hire. One pressing a right by the sword looks for another fief it could take instead. The claimants win if they **take the fief within 72 days and hold it for 10**; otherwise the house keeps it. Then all come back under the oath, and the winner keeps the fief. Any other fief one side took from the other while it lasted goes back to its house, as custom asks, though now and then the taker refuses (less likely an honourable or generous one); a house that keeps one makes an enemy of the house it took it from, which keeps its right to it. If it is you who took it, you choose.
- **A foe at the walls.** If an enemy besieges a place of the realm during the feud, the ruler calls the houses to arms if the crown has the influence for it (if you are the ruler, you choose). With no call, the feud goes on. With a call, the two houses at feud answer first, each for its whole side, and nearly always they heed the crown: an honourable head all the more, a calculating one a little less. If a head refuses, each house of its side decides for itself, and a bitter, honourless house may, rarely, go over to the invader with its lands. A house that fights on while its rival came home has three days before it is held a traitor. When all have laid down their arms, the feud waits until the realm has been safe for a while, and takes up again if the grudge is still there. If lords of both sides fight side by side against the invader meanwhile, the two houses may make it up and end the feud: a **40%** chance, **+10%** for each level of Mercy and **+5%** for each level of Generosity of either head (never below 10% nor above 80%); if they do not, it goes on. A house that made it up while holding the fief keeps it. And a feud that ends with nobody the winner while the claimants already hold the fief counts as won: the fief is theirs. A feud already under way is not stopped by a war alone, only by a foe at the walls.
- **More companies in a feud.** A noble house of the AI usually leads fewer companies than your house or a free company of the same standing. While it is out of its realm for a feud, on either side, it may lead as many as they do, so most houses can put one or two more in the field. Each still needs a lord of the house to lead it. Once the house is home again, the companies raised for the feud are disbanded: their lords ride home and their men join the garrison of the house's nearest castle or town (or the realm's, if it holds none); their stores are lost.
- **Other quarrels fall away.** A house that rides out to a feud, on either side, leaves behind whatever else it was part of in the realm. A charter or a reckoning goes on without it, under its strongest house if it was the one leading it, or comes to nothing; a plot against the throne it led falls apart. A reckoning against a house that rides out waits for it: it neither moves nor ends while the house is away, and takes up where it left off once the house is back. If you are asked to a feud, or offer your sword, while your house is part of such a cause, you are told what you would leave behind.
- **Winning houses over.** While a feud is prepared, its claimants look for more friends; once it is declared, both sides do. Every few days the head of each side of the AI asks the house most likely to say yes. A house rides only for a cause of its own: its head is a good friend of the one who asks, or on bad terms with the other side's head, or it holds a grudge against the other house, or it is honourable and the one who asks has the better right. With none, it stays home. Silver does not buy it: once it has said yes, the head who asked pays it a compensation for keeping its men in the field (if it joined while the feud was still being prepared, once the feud begins), as one realm does when it calls an ally to a war (whatever he can spare). A house that says no is left alone for a while. Nobody is courted while the feud waits for the peace or a foe is at the walls. You may be written to with such a request, and told what you would be paid; if you head a side, you may ask the head of any house of the realm in conversation, and you pay the same way. A house that joins earns the other side's ill will.
- **The others remember.** A house that left the realm alone with a foe at the walls, or went over to the foe, earns grievances from every other house.

A realm torn by a feud looks weaker to its neighbours, who are readier to make war on it. Open the [Factions tab](civil-war.md#the-factions-tab) to see a feud's two sides face to face.

## Asking a ruler to step down

A [plot against the throne](civil-war.md) that has gathered a good part of the realm asks the ruler to lay down the crown before it rises. An AI ruler only thinks it over when the plot has most of the realm behind it, far more than it needs to ask (below that he always refuses, and the plot may ask again as soon as it is strong enough), and even then a ruler with a strong claim may well refuse; a weak claim makes him likelier to agree. A ruler newly crowned is left in peace for his first year. If he does agree, the realm chooses a new ruler by its [own law](succession.md), and the plot's houses, its leader among them, vote together for the house the plot would crown, its [pretender](civil-war.md#who-it-would-crown). If he refuses, the lords hold it against him, and the plot goes on.

If you are the ruler, you are asked too, with no year of grace and from 35% of the realm's strength (not again for 90 days after you refuse). You can step down and stay as a vassal with your lands, or refuse and take what comes.

## Alliances between houses

Two houses of a realm may become allies, the ruler's and yours as any other.

- **By marriage.** When a head of a house, or a child or a brother or sister of one, weds someone of another house of the same realm, the two houses are allied, if their heads were on good terms before the wedding. The alliance rests on that marriage: if one of the couple dies, it holds if another couple of the two houses is still wed, or as a sworn pact if their heads are good friends; otherwise it is over.
- **By a pact.** Heads of houses who are good friends may swear to stand together, now and then, rarely. A head of the AI may write to you to offer it; you may also ask a head of house who is your good friend in conversation (*(House alliance)*). If he says no, give it a couple of months: ask again sooner and he only tells you his answer has not changed.
- **What it does.** Allies are called to arms in a feud (see above). In a plot against the crown, a charter or a reckoning, a house leans more to join one an ally of it leads (or, for a plot, one that would crown an ally), and far less to join one against an ally of it: the crown, or the house a reckoning is after. Allied to both, neither counts.
- **When it ends.** When a house leaves the realm (also by riding over to the rebels of a civil war), when the two end on opposite sides of a feud or one joins a reckoning against the other, when the heads of a sworn pact fall out, or when an ally refuses a call to arms.
- **Weddings.** Lords of the AI marry within their own realm far more than abroad (a wedding across realms is a quarter as likely; in the base game, half), and so do the matches offered to your house.

Your allies are listed in the [House tab](house-tab.md) of the clan screen, under *In the realm*.

## You and the factions

Talk to the head of another house of your realm:

These talks are under one line of the game's *"There is something I'd like to discuss."*: **"There are matters of the realm I would speak of, away from other ears. (Plots, factions and alliances)"**, shown only when one of them is open to you. Every line the mod adds to a lord's talk ends with a tag in brackets saying what it belongs to: *(Plot against the crown)*, *(Factions)*, *(Fief vote)*, *(Feud)*, *(Squire path)*, *(Manhunter path)*, *(Marshal)*, *(Royal court)* and so on.

- **What news from the realms of Calradia? (News of the realms)** Any lord, of any kingdom, unless he dislikes you: pick a realm and he tells you who rules it and how strong it is, its wars (with whom, for how long, who declared it and the reason its lords gave, and how it goes), its alliances, and its troubles within as far as they are known abroad: a civil war, a feud between two of its houses, a charter or a reckoning pressed in the open, lords demanding the ruler step down, talk of a plot (never who is in it), traitors being judged.
- **What news of the realm, my friend?** Any lord who is your friend, of any kingdom (not its ruler), tells you who is pressing whom: the factions, their aims, who stands with them, how far along they are, whether there is a darker plot against the throne, and what his own house holds against the crown. A good friend tells you more; an honourable one keeps the secrets of his own cause unless you are in it; a calculating one may remember more for a consideration. When a faction a friend is in comes to a head, he may write to you.
- **Join** a lord's charter or reckoning, or **leave** it.
- **Start a charter**, or **start a reckoning**: you choose the house, then the fief, then who should have it. (The houses of the AI may start one against your house too: you hear the rumour first, then the news when it comes out in the open.) Win other lords over one by one; how likely they are to agree is said in words.
- **Tell the ruler.** If you know of a faction still in whispers, you can take it to the ruler. He acts at once, and thanks you. Whoever told you about it will find out.
- **Ask the ruler to step down**, if you lead a plot with most of the realm behind it.

When you lead a faction, you bring its laws or its claim before the council yourself, from the kingdom screen; its lords vote with you. Your factions, and those that put their case to you as ruler, have their own entry in the journal.

## Not too many civil wars

In the AI kingdoms there is only one civil war at a time, and some time must pass between one and the next. A plot or a charter that could rise waits, and keeps pressing its case. Civil wars in your own kingdom do not count towards this.

## Turn it off

In the [settings file](../settings.md), set to `false`:

- `FactionsEnabled`: all of it (the plots and civil wars of before stay, alone);
- `FactionGrievancesEnabled`: the wrongs houses remember; `FactionGrievanceRelationsEnabled`: wrongs between AI houses souring their heads; `FactionGrievanceRelationsWithPlayer`: the same against your house; `FactionCrownGrievanceRelationsEnabled`: wrongs against the crown costing goodwill toward the ruler; `FactionAbandonedGrievanceEnabled`: armies that came near a besieged fief and did nothing are blamed (off: any lord of the realm near the fief when it falls spares the crown, as before); `FactionMarshalBlameEnabled`: the Marshal sharing the crown's blame; `PleaToRelieveEnabled`: the pleas of the lords of your host;
- `FactionCharterEnabled`, `FactionReckoningEnabled`, `FactionAbdicationEnabled`, `FactionConfidantsEnabled`: each part on its own;
- `FiefRightsReckoningNeedsRight` (on the [Rights to fiefs](fief-rights.md) page): a reckoning needs a right;
- `FactionFeudsEnabled`: reckonings taking up arms; `FactionFeudDefyCouncilEnabled`: an AI reckoning taking up arms after the council's no; `FactionFeudPressRightEnabled`: a right pressed by the sword; `FactionFeudPlayerJoinEnabled`: your sword in a feud (letters, offers, hire); `FactionFeudGiveBackEnabled`: fiefs given back after a feud; `FactionStripRebelsEnabled`: a house refusing a fief taken by a reckoning's vote; `RevokeFeudEnabled`: a house holding such a fief by force (it yields instead); `ReckoningsWaitForPeaceEnabled`: reckonings waiting for the peace (and no vassal putting a fief to the council in wartime); `FeudAllyCallEnabled`: allies called to a feud; `FeudCourtEnabled`: courting houses into a feud; `FeudAiDefiesBanEnabled`: an AI house defying the crown's ban; `FeudPartySizeEnabled`: bigger companies in a feud; `FeudStrengthCountsHiresEnabled`: what a purse could hire counted in a feud's strength;
- `HouseAlliancesEnabled`: alliances between houses; `AlliesInCausesEnabled`: allies in plots and causes; `MarriageOtherRealmEnabled`: fewer weddings across realms;
- `FactionsInAiRealms`: factions in the AI kingdoms; `FactionAiInternalWarLimit`: the limit on civil wars.
