# Factions and grievances

Lords do not only plot against the throne. They remember what the crown has done to them, and they band together behind a cause: a charter to bind the ruler's hands, the fall of a house that has grown too great, or a new ruler. A faction tries the council first, the lawful way. Only when the council fails it does it put its case to the ruler, and only when the ruler refuses does it think of arms.

All of this happens in every kingdom, not only yours. You can join a faction, lead one, betray one to the crown, or face them from the throne.

## Lords remember

Every house keeps a short list of wrongs, each with a name, a place and a date. Lords speak of them in conversation, and the worst of them show in the [kingdom screen's Factions tab](civil-war.md#the-factions-tab).

| What happened | Against whom |
|---|---|
| A town or castle of theirs fell to a siege while an army of the realm, led by another house, lingered within a day's ride for half a day or more, free to come, and never fought for it | the house that led that army (the crown, if it was the ruler's army or his family's) |
| A town or castle of theirs fell to a siege, no army of the realm marched to it or lingered near it, and no lord of another house fought for it or stood within its walls | the crown, and the house of the realm's [Marshal](royal-court.md#the-marshals-burden) |
| A conquered fief they stood for was given to another house (twice running weighs double) | the crown, and a little the house that got it |
| The ruler's own house took a conquered fief they stood for | the crown |
| The council voted to take a fief from them for a reckoning | the reckoning's houses (its head in full, the others by half), and the crown only if the ruler wanted it (his house voted for it, or he carried it over the council's head) |
| The ruler took a fief of theirs by decree, or their lord by his demand | the crown, or their lord (and the house that asked for it, if the ruler backed someone) |
| The ruler gave a fief of theirs to their rivals by decree, in a feud | the crown |
| The realm bought its peace with tribute | the crown |
| A kinsman was executed | the crown, or the house that did it |
| The crown shielded a house they wanted brought down, or spurned their charter | the crown |
| The crown took a conquered fief for itself while a charter forbade it | the crown |
| The ruler's house holds more towns and castles than its due (see below) and more than **30%** of the realm's | the crown |
| A vassal house holds more towns and castles than its due and more than **20%** of the realm's | that house (not the crown) |
| They came to your court to ask for a fief of yours they claim by right, and you refused | the crown |

### New wrongs remembered {#new-wrongs-remembered}

| What happened | Against whom |
|---|---|
| The crown gave a town or castle of theirs to the enemy as part of the price of a [peace](war-and-peace.md) | the crown |
| They had to yield a town or castle to the crown at the peace that ended a [civil war](civil-war.md) | the crown |
| A house pledged its vote to theirs at the peace that ended a civil war, and gave it to another when the lords chose a ruler | that house |
| The crown rebuked them before the court for their part in a failed [rising](realm-houses.md) | the crown |
| A town or castle of theirs fell, and their own [lord](titles.md) brought his host close by and never fought for it, or never came though he was free to | their lord |
| They asked their lord to come to their aid in a feud, he would not though he was free, and the place fell | their lord |
| They were attacked in a feud, and you, their lord, stood aside | your house |
| A vacant duchy or ulus they hold land in went to another house | the crown, and a little the house that got it |
| They are a duke and the ruler keeps a town of their duchy for himself: held for as long as that lasts, and gone the day it ends | the crown |
| They are a duke, a noyan or a count, and another house of the realm holds land of their duchy, ulus or county without answering to them: held for as long as that lasts | that house |
| Another house won their duchy or ulus from them in a feud for the title | that house (the crown, if the ruler decreed it) |
| A vassal of theirs rode against them in a feud | that house |
| The emperor took the command of their theme from them, or the khan took back their ulus for treason | the crown |
| The emperor took a fief of theirs on another house's [petition](empire.md), or turned their own petition away | the crown |

**Too much land in one hand.** Every 30 days, while the ruler's house holds more towns and castles than its due and more than 30% of the realm's, every other house (not the ruler's kin) holds it a little more against the crown: 2 for each fief beyond its due. A vassal house with more than its due that are more than 20% of the realm's is begrudged the same way, against that house alone (its own kin excepted). A house's due grows with its tier: one town or castle up to tier 2, then one more for each tier (2 at tier 3, up to 5 at tier 6); the crown is due two more than that. It is one grudge per house that keeps growing while the hoard lasts, and fades like any other once it ends. The goodwill it costs is another matter, and fixed: while the hoard lasts, the head of each of those houses thinks 5 less of the ruler (or of the head of that house) for each town or castle beyond its due, looked at every day. Take one more and it costs 5 more; hold one fewer and those 5 come back; once the house is back within its due (or under its share of the realm), all of it comes back. It never piles up: one town or castle too many costs 5 with each house, however long it lasts, even if both houses begrudge each other. When a house gets a new head (the old one dies, retires or leaves it), what was taken from the old head is given back first, so the new head does not inherit it; the new head is then charged once like anyone else. It counts the same for you, as ruler or vassal, and a hoarding house is the natural target of a [reckoning](#the-reckoning-of-a-house). Your own house's towns and castles against its due, and whether they are begrudged, are under Your lands in the [House tab](house-tab.md) of the clan screen; every house's, beside its tier in the Clans tab of the kingdom screen ("6 of 4, begrudged").

**Grudges between houses.** When one house of an AI realm comes to hold a wrong against another, their heads fall out at once: a house passed over in a share-out thinks less of the house that got the fief; a house that left the realm alone with a foe at the walls during a feud loses the goodwill of every other house, and much more if it defies the crown to the end or goes over to the foe. The same holds against your house: a house you beat to a share-out, or leave alone against a foe in a feud, thinks less of you; and if your house defies the crown to the end, or goes over to the foe, in a feud, every house of the realm turns against you at once, as it would against any other. Your own feelings are never moved for you: whom you like is up to you.

**Who came, and who fought.** While a fief is besieged, the realm watches which armies come near it, and whether they really helped: one of their parties fought at that siege (breaking the besiegers from outside, sallying out with the garrison, holding the walls against the assault) or rode into the fief. An army that lingered close and did nothing is what the house remembers when the fief falls, and its head thinks less of whoever led it, you included. Only the hours it was free to come count: not while it was besieging another fief, fighting a battle, relieving or holding another besieged fief of the realm, still gathering its lords, or already marching to this one or chasing its besiegers. An army still on its way when the walls fall is not blamed. If the house had asked you in person to relieve the fief (see below) and you answered, your answer and what you did count instead. A lone lord riding past no longer spares the crown; only a lord who fought for the fief does. A siege that is lifted leaves no grudge.

**A plea from your host.** When you lead an army and a fief of a house riding in it is besieged not far off, one of its lords (the head of the house, if they ride with you) rides up beside you in their war gear and, as one who marches under your banner, asks you to lead the army to its relief: once per siege and house, however many of them ride with you. Refuse and the house thinks less of you. Promise and it watches: if the fief falls and your host never fought for it nor rode into it, your word is remembered as broken, and that costs more than refusing; if you did help, the house is grateful when the siege ends, whether the fief held or not. If the siege is lifted without you, nobody minds. Help after refusing is remembered kindly too.

**Grudges against the crown.** A wrong a house lays at its ruler's door (a fief lost with no help sent, passed over, the king's share, a shameful peace, a ruler who shields their enemy or spurns them, a broken charter, a fief given away by the ruler's decree) also costs a little of its head's goodwill toward the ruler (a town of a duke's duchy kept by the crown costs none: it is how things stand, not something done to him), whether the ruler is of the AI or you. A little each time: a ruler who fails the same house again and again makes an enemy of it, and an enemy of the ruler is who starts a plot. A good [Chancellor](royal-court.md#the-offices) softens it: the more charming, the less goodwill the wrongs cost, down to half only with a truly exceptional one (a small wrong may cost none at all), though the house remembers the wrong just the same. A fief taken by the council's vote or an executed kinsman costs that goodwill the way the game already does, and a rebuke before the court costs it once, when it is given; neither costs it twice. Your goodwill toward a ruler, as a vassal, is never moved for you.

**Great realms breed rancour.** In a kingdom grown beyond its fair share of Calradia, every wrong against the crown weighs more when it is born, up to twice as much in a kingdom of twice its fair share, and costs the ruler that much more goodwill. The lords of such a kingdom also band together more readily: they lean further to every faction, and a smaller grudge is enough to begin a Reckoning (see [Great realms, restless lords](civil-war.md#great-realms-restless-lords)). Grudges between houses do not grow with the realm.

Wrongs fade with time (an executed kinsman, much more slowly). When a new house takes the throne, the old grievances against the crown weigh only half.

A house's grievances make it readier to join a [plot against the throne](civil-war.md) and any faction against the crown. So does serving a ruler of another [faith](faiths.md), and a ruler who refused to step down when the lords asked.

## The Charter {#the-charter}

Houses with grievances against the crown write a charter of up to three clauses:

- to strike down the crown's strongest laws in force (such as Royal Privilege, Sacred Majesty or War Tax);
- to make law one of the lords' own (such as the Senate or the Lords' Privy Council);
- **fiefs to the realm**: if the ruler has been keeping conquests for his own house, that for a while the ruler's house takes no share of conquered fiefs.

The faction brings its laws before the council one by one, and all its lords vote together. If the council turns it down, or a clause cannot be voted, it puts the whole charter to the ruler and waits for an answer.

The ruler may **sign it** (the laws change and each lord forgets the heaviest of his grievances), **offer its leader a purse** (if he takes it he goes quiet for a good while, and the faction looks for another head; he may send it back, and then the ultimatum stands), **strike at the leader** (take his office, or a fief of his by [decree](royal-court.md#taking-a-fief-from-a-vassal), never his house's last), or **refuse**. Striking does not end the ultimatum. When the time runs out without a yes, the charter rises if more than half the realm (55% of its strength; 40% if you lead it) stands behind it and the realm is not already in a civil war, even while it is at war with another kingdom (an AI charter in a realm that stands weak in its wars rises far less often: see [Quarrels in a time of war](#quarrels-in-a-time-of-war)); if not, it asks once more after a while, and the second time it breaks up bitter. AI rulers seldom sign: charters get their laws mostly by winning the council's votes. A charter's rising is a [civil war](civil-war.md) in which the ruler keeps the throne if he loses, and signs. If you lead it and it is its last asking, the window that asks you to raise its banner says so, and its other button reads **"Let the charter go"**.

- **The laws of the crown.** Where [High Crown Authority](crown-laws.md#high-crown-authority) is law, a return to Autonomous Vassals is always a charter's first demand; a Normal or High [tax obligation](crown-laws.md#the-tax-obligation) brought down is its second. Each vote or seal lowers the tax one step, and the clause is won only when the tax is truly below what the charter asked.
- **The law of succession.** Where the ruler has brought in a [law of succession](succession.md#the-laws-of-succession) against the realm's custom, the houses hold it against the crown (*"the crown has changed the very law by which it passes, against the custom of our fathers"*), and a charter may demand the old law back. It can ask nothing else of the succession, and only while the new law is resented: once the new law has become the custom, the charter drops that demand, and a charter left with nothing to ask is over.
- **A sealed charter binds the crown for a good while**, however it was won: a ruler who undoes one of its laws in that time breaks the charter, and the houses that sealed it remember. An AI ruler does not do it.
- **If you lead it**, the crown never buys you without asking: a window, **"The crown's purse"**, offers you its gold (**"Take the purse"**: you leave the charter and no lord hears you on a charter or a reckoning for a while; or **"Send it back"**). Your charter keeps time as an AI one does: if you let a long while pass without putting one of its laws to the council when you could, it counts as a vote lost, and it comes before the ruler or fades. A lord of the charter reminds you a week before.
- **If you are the ruler**, you may win houses back from a charter set against you, by the same odds an AI ruler has: speak to the head of a house that sealed it (*"Your seal is on a charter set against my crown. Take it off..."*), once in a while for each charter, never its head.

When the head of the house leading a charter or a reckoning dies, the heir leads it on with the house; it passes to another house only if this one can no longer lead it (it left the realm, took the crown, rode out to a feud and so on). The same holds for your heir. If the cause then falls to your house as the strongest left in it, a window asks whether you take its head; if you would rather not, another house leads it if any will.

**Not in a civil war.** No charter or reckoning can begin while the realm is in a civil war or in the calm after one, yours either: you are told, and the Factions tab says so.

## The Reckoning of a house {#the-reckoning-of-a-house}

Lords band together to take a fief from another house of the realm, but only a fief the house that leads them has a [right](fief-rights.md) to. The fief is always for that house: if another house takes the lead, the fief is for it. A grudge against that house, or its having grown too great (a quarter of the realm's strength or more), is what moves them; a firm right is reason enough on its own for a house that has no love for the holder. The ruler's own fiefs are never the target. A realm holds at most three reckonings at a time. **A lord never joins a reckoning against his own vassal**, AI or you, not even one another vassal of his leads: his only road to a vassal's fief is to [demand it](royal-court.md#taking-a-fief-from-a-vassal).

**Never in the Empire.** In a realm of the Empire (the three Empires, and any realm of imperial culture) the land is the emperor's to give: lords do not band together to take it from one another, neither the AI nor you. A house with a right takes it to the emperor as a [petition](empire.md) instead.

Once it is declared (its houses hold a fifth of the realm's strength, with at least one house besides its head), it proposes taking the fief in the council, and its houses vote together. The council votes on taking a fief from a house only for a reckoning; the ruler and a lord [take one by decree or demand](royal-court.md#taking-a-fief-from-a-vassal).

- **If the council says yes**, the house yields (the fief is shared out, and the reckoning's house brings its claim to the share-out), breaks with the realm, or bars its gates (see below).
- **If the council says no**, an AI reckoning may take up arms; otherwise it puts its case to the ruler, who has a week to answer.
- **The ruler has two answers**: **back it** and order the house out by decree (the fief goes straight to the reckoning's house, with no share-out), or **refuse** and shield the holder. Backing one the council voted for costs the ruler only the goodwill of the house that loses the fief; backing one the council turned down also costs **10** with every other vassal house, except the reckoning's own and unless the claimant's right is firm. As ruler, your window offers **"Take their side: order the {house} out by decree"** and **"Refuse"**.
- **An AI ruler** usually backs a reckoning the council voted for, less readily if he is fond of the holder. One the council voted down he refuses, unless its house holds a firm right to the fief, he holds its head very dear, or he hates the holder; the more of these, the likelier. He never backs it against a holder he holds very dear.
- **A refusal** is remembered by every house of the reckoning as the crown shielding its foe, and the reckoning may take up arms or break up.
- **A reckoning wins only if the fief truly reaches the house it was for.** If the share-out gives it to another, the house broke away with it, a foreign army took it or the crown kept it, it ends with no victor.

**A small realm spares its houses.** In a realm with fewer towns and castles than its [fair share](civil-war.md#how-large-a-kingdom-is), the AI lords back a reckoning's vote to take a fief less strongly, the reckoning's own houses too: a small realm cannot afford to lose a house over one fief. The smaller the realm, the more they hold back; a lord may stand aside altogether. Your own vote is yours as always, and a house that promised you its voice as ruler keeps its word. When the council leaves the fief with its house because of this, the news may carry the words of a lord who held back, such as *"We cannot spare a single sword while the realm is this small."* What comes after a "no" (the demand to the ruler, a feud) is unchanged.

**When you start one**, tell a lord *"There is a fief in this realm held against a better right than its holder's."*, then pick the house and the town or castle. Only fiefs your house has a right to are offered, each with how firm it is; the fief is for your house. How likely the lord is to agree is said in words, and depends on their grudge against that house and their bonds with it and with you. You can start one against a house led by a kinsman of the ruler too. You bring its vote before the council yourself (the kingdom screen's **Annex**) once it is declared and has a house beside yours; until then the button is greyed with the reason. Like a charter, your reckoning keeps time: a long while without a vote counts as a vote lost. AI reckonings go after the fief their leader has the firmest right to. A reckoning whose houses no longer have a right to anything the house holds comes to an end. An AI house presses a claim [by marriage](fief-rights.md#a-claim-by-marriage) only when the two heads are on bad terms; you may press yours at any time.

**Against one's own blood.** An AI house joins a reckoning against its kin or in-laws only when it is on very bad terms with them and on very good terms with the reckoning's head, even when you ask it; you decide for yourself.

**What the stripped house does.** A house that loses a fief to a reckoning's vote has three ways to take it, and its head's character decides:

- **It breaks with the realm**, taking its lands with it and founding its [league](realm-houses.md#a-house-that-breaks-with-its-realm). Since it could bar its gates instead, an AI house seldom does, unless it has another king to go to: a ruler at war with its realm whom it likes, while it has no love for its own; then it may go over to him with all its fiefs.
- **It holds the fief by force.** It bars its gates against the council's verdict, and the reckoning takes up arms against it in a feud (see below); the ruler lets the swords settle it. A brave head is likelier to do this, more so against a weaker reckoning; an honourable one bows to the council, and a calculating one or a friend of the ruler thinks twice. **If no feud can be fought** (a foe at the walls, the realm already holding as many feuds as it can, a reckoning without the men to reach the walls), the house keeps the fief, and the reckoning takes the council's vote up to the ruler. The same if the feud is declared but nobody draws a sword in the end.
- **It yields**, and the fief is shared out.

Under [High Crown Authority](crown-laws.md#high-crown-authority) no vote of the council puts a house out of its fief: the house keeps it, and the matter goes up to the throne.

If it is your house, a window, **"The council's verdict"**, asks you: **"Bow to the council"**, **"Bar the gates"** or **"Renounce your oath"**, and **"Raise the plot's banner"** if a plot you are sworn to can rise over it. What you cannot do is shown greyed with the reason.

**A lord stands by his vassal.** In a reckoning's vote to take a town or castle from a house, the house's own lord votes to leave it with them, as hard as a reckoning's houses vote to take it: he would lose a vassal. The less he likes them, the less he stands by them, and not at all if he hates them or bears them a deep grudge. Not the ruler (who judges as the crown), nor a lord they hold of only as the crown. Your own vote is yours.

In a long peace, the realm's [assembly](assembly.md) hears the wrongs between houses of the last war and the crown's wrongs a house asks it to make good; a feud is never brought before it.

## Quarrels in a time of war {#quarrels-in-a-time-of-war}

A war with another kingdom no longer holds back the quarrels of a realm. Plots and charters may rise, reckonings bring their votes and their demands, feuds are declared and fought, and a house stripped of a fief may break with the realm or bar its gates, all in the middle of a war, as in peace.

**A realm that stands weak** in its wars is far less ready to turn on itself. While it is at war with another kingdom and is either **losing** (much weaker than its foes, and behind in at least one of its wars) or **small** (fewer than half the towns and castles of the average realm), every chance its AI lords have to start a war within is about **3 in 10** of what it would be: a plot or a charter rising, a feud taken up, a house breaking with the realm or holding a fief by force. A realm grown far beyond its fair share is never held weak, even while it loses. At peace, or at war and not weak, nothing changes. The [coup of a strategos](empire.md) is never held back by this.

- Your own choices are yours: you may raise your plot, press your right or answer a vote as you will. Only what is left to your house's character (whether it dares defy a sentence, say) weighs the realm's weakness, as for any house.
- **A charter** of the AI whose time runs out in a weak realm rises only about 3 times in 10; otherwise it asks again.
- **A feud being prepared** whose lords chose to take up arms while the realm was still sound weighs its weakness once more when it is ready to declare. Held back, it waits, ready, and weighs it again every 10 days, or declares as soon as the realm stands sound again, within the 90 days a feud's preparations may last. If you ride with them, their head tells you so in a letter.
- **A battle under way** delays a rising only a few hours, a day at most: a plot or a charter waits for its houses' battles to end, then rises; its lords leave the sieges and armies other houses lead. A feud about to begin waits for its houses' battles, or an assault on their walls, to end. If you lead a plot whose men are in a battle, your own **Raise the banner** is greyed meanwhile: *"the banner waits until the fighting is over."*
- With a foe besieging a town or castle of the realm, no feud begins, as before.

## When a reckoning takes up arms: feuds between houses {#when-a-reckoning-takes-up-arms-feuds-between-houses}

A reckoning that the ruler refused may not leave it there. When its last demand runs out, its houses may take up arms against the house they would bring down, to take the fief by force. An AI reckoning the council turns down may do the same, unwilling to abide its word. A reckoning that won its vote also takes up arms when the house [holds the fief by force](#the-reckoning-of-a-house) against the council; the sides are drawn the same way, but the ruler is not asked and lets them fight it out. If such a feud never begins (the claimants give up while still gathering their men, their leading house is gone, or you lead the reckoning and let it go), the house that barred its gates keeps the fief, and the reckoning takes the council's vote up to the ruler. A realm can have five feuds at a time, but no house rides in two, nor is one fief fought over twice. If you lead the reckoning, you decide: turned down by the council, you are asked whether to take up arms. If a reckoning's vote goes against your own house, you may **bar the gates** and hold the fief by force, as a house of the AI may; the reckoning then comes for it.

**A right pressed by the sword.** A feud needs no reckoning. Now and then a house of the AI with a [right](fief-rights.md) to a fief another house of its realm holds sends that house its defiance and comes to take it, if its side is strong enough, counting the allies that would answer its call, the houses with a cause to join it, and what its purse could hire. You may do the same, in any of [three ways](#three-ways-to-press-a-right); the same houses are out of reach for you (kin, close friends, the ruler's house), but whether your side is strong enough is yours to judge. A right to a [title](titles.md#claims-to-a-title) is pressed the same way, in a [feud for the title](titles.md#a-feud-for-a-title). A firm right, a bold head with little honour, and bad blood with the holder all make it likelier. Kin and friends never come to this, and neither does the ruler's own house, nor a lord against a house sworn to him: he can only [demand its fief](titles.md#a-lord-takes-back-a-vassals-fief). Under [High Crown Authority](crown-laws.md#high-crown-authority) no right is pressed by the sword. If the fief is yours, you are asked whether to fight or give it up; if the realm is yours, you answer as ruler.

**Never in the Empire.** In a realm of the Empire a lord who takes up arms against another is a rebel against the throne: no feud is fought there, by any road. A right is laid before the emperor as a [petition](empire.md) instead.

- **Allies called to arms.** Each [ally](#alliances-between-houses) of the head of either side is called first, and most answer; an honourable one, or one who likes the caller well, all the more. One allied to both heads stays out. An ally who refuses breaks the alliance, and the house that called holds it against him. If you are the ally called, you are asked.
- **Kin and friends who stay home.** A house that is kin or a close friend of a side's head and will not ride when called loses a little of that head's regard, you and the AI alike (bound to both sides and riding with neither, you lose nothing).
- **The two sides.** Each of the reckoning's houses may ride with its leader, the bolder the likelier (with a right pressed by the sword, the claimant rides alone at first). A house of the reckoning that stays home when its head takes up arms loses a little of that head's regard, you and the AI alike (not if you said you would ride and were then taken prisoner or shut in). The house under attack is joined by its kin and its close friends; an AI house that holds the fief always fights for it. Other houses ride only if they are kin or close friends of one side: one bound to both goes with the one it likes best, and a house bound to neither stays home. Now and then an honourable house with no ties to either side, and no enmity with the holder, stands with the holder, when the claimants' [right](fief-rights.md) to the fief is weaker than the holder's; the more honourable, the likelier. A noble house with no town or castle may ride on either side like any other; a [landed knight](landed-knights.md) takes no side of his own, but rides in his lord's host, and a house that loses its last town or castle in the feud stays on its side to the end.
- **Your part in it.** When a feud breaks out in your realm, the head of a house that is your kin or your friend writes to ask your help: the claimants to ride with them, the holder to stand with them. Bound to both, you get both letters and choose one side, or neither. At any time while the feud lasts you may also offer your sword yourself: speak to the head of either house (or, while the head is a prisoner, to a grown lord of the house), or send an envoy with the offer. A head who likes you little turns you down; a calculating one takes help where he finds it. Joining earns you the ill will of the other side's head. If you serve the realm as a mercenary, you offer your company instead: the house pays you once, if its coffers can bear it, you leave the realm's service (an unfinished contract counts as broken), and you ride with its host as a sellsword, sharing its wars (the realm's foes are its foes), until the feud is done, when they let you go. As anywhere, a sellsword is given no fief: a castle your company storms goes to the house that leads the host.
- **The ruler's answer.** The ruler can let them be, forbid the feud, or stand with one side (with the claimants, he gives them the fief by decree). A ruler of the AI nearly always lets them be. Forbidding it or taking a side costs a great deal of influence, and only a ruler whose right to rule the lords hold secure can do either. **The crown respects a firm right:** when the claimants hold a firm [right](fief-rights.md) to the fief (or a firm claim to the title), the ruler, you included, may only let them settle it or stand with them; he can neither forbid the feud nor shield the holder. Your warnings before you take up arms say what the crown may still do. Standing with the claimants is a [decree](royal-court.md#taking-a-fief-from-a-vassal) and costs what a decree costs (nothing with the claimants, who asked for it); the holder answers it as any decree. **Defying the crown:** a leader whom the crown forbade, or who sees the crown stand with the holder, may defy it and break with the realm, by a weighing of valour, honour and regard for the ruler: a lord who loves his ruler almost never does. You may defy it too.
- **The fight.** For the length of the feud each side rides out as a host of its own, under the banner of its leading house, named after it and its realm (*Host of the dey Molarn (Vlandia)*): a kingdom in all but name, with the realm's laws and customs, that raises armies, lays sieges and rides to the relief of its walls like any other. The houses keep their lands, and their lords are still sworn to the crown: they still count as the realm's own, pay their dues, swear the oath to a new ruler, stand for vacant titles and keep their rights. The two hosts are at war only with each other, and with the realm's foes as before. No other kingdom may make war or peace with a host, nor ally with it, and no lord or sellsword of the AI takes service in one. A host holds no council: a fief it takes stays with the house that took it until the feud is settled. When the feud is over, the hosts are broken up.
- **To arms.** When a feud breaks out, both sides call up their men: for some days, lords whose companies are thin make recruiting their first care, riding to the towns and villages that would give them men and paying for them as always; the garrisons are left to hold the walls. Each host also hires free companies of sellswords, the nearest first, as long as its richest house can pay them; they ride with it until the feud is over. Once the feud is declared, the lords of both sides may also hire the [companies for hire](free-companies.md) of the taverns, as any lord at war does, if their purses can bear the whole term, taking turns so that neither side takes them all; nobody hires while a feud is still being prepared in silence. If you head a side, the nearest company you can afford offers you its service once, when the feud begins (**"Hire them (… denars)"** or **"Not now"**); after that, hire your swords by [envoy](envoys.md). A company so hired serves its whole term, even if the feud ends first. The lords of a host can gather an army with lighter companies than a realm would ask, and march it on the walls. Before the walls of the fief in dispute they need no more strength than the walls hold, where a realm's army would want twice as much, since a lesser house can look for little relief. Nor do they storm it while its walls stand: they let their engines batter them down first, for a week or so at most, and then storm it all the same. While the feud is fought, every lord's company of the houses riding in it holds a fifth more men than usual (yours too); once it is over they are back to their usual size, and the extra men drift away.
- **Gathering before the defiance.** A house of the AI that means to take up arms does not send its defiance at once. Its lords and their friends first gather their men in silence, making recruiting their first care, and declare only once their companies are nearly full and the walls are within their reach. If the men cannot be found within about a month, the quarrel is let be, and nobody need ever know how close it came; however long a foe at the walls holds them back, preparations end after 90 days in all. A feud prepared in secret gets in nobody's way: the house it aims at knows nothing and goes about its business, and it does not count among the realm's feuds until it is declared. If its leader or its target is drawn into another feud first, the preparations are given up.
- **No hopeless feuds.** A house of the AI does not take up arms over a fief its side could never besiege, even with every man called up and every sellsword and company it could afford; a side that can bring about two thirds of what the siege needs may try, counting on the men it will still raise and hire. One pressing a right by the sword looks for another fief it could take instead. The claimants win if they **take the fief within 72 days and hold it for 10**; otherwise the house keeps it. If they hold it when the time is up, they fight on until their 10 days are full or they lose it. A side whose leading house loses every town and castle it held loses the feud once the other side has held the last of them for 10 days, not on the day it falls (the other houses of a side fight on without land). The journal says so when the feud begins, for the claimants, the holder and their allies alike, and shows the days the claimants have held it, the time left, and the fief marked on the map. (A feud for a title is won another way: see [A feud for a title](titles.md#a-feud-for-a-title).) Then all come back under the oath, and the winner keeps the fief. What a house takes while the feud is being fought does not become its seat, does not change its lord, pays no dues and gives no title. Any other fief one side took from the other while it lasted goes back to its house, as custom asks, though now and then the taker refuses (less likely an honourable or generous one); a house that keeps one makes an enemy of the house it took it from, which keeps its right to it. If it is you who took it, you choose.
- **A foe at the walls.** If an enemy besieges a place of the realm during the feud, the crown may call the houses to arms: see [The crown's call to arms](#the-crowns-call-to-arms) below. A feud already under way is not stopped by a war alone; when the realm declares a new war, the crown may [summon its host](#the-host-summoned-for-a-war-abroad).
- **More companies in a feud.** A noble house of the AI usually leads fewer companies than your house or a free company of the same standing. While it is out of its realm for a feud, on either side, it may lead as many as they do, so most houses can put one or two more in the field. Each still needs a lord of the house to lead it. Once the house is home again, the companies raised for the feud are disbanded: their lords ride home and their men join the garrison of the house's nearest castle or town (or the realm's, if it holds none); their stores are lost.
- **Other quarrels fall away.** A house that rides out to a feud, on either side, leaves behind whatever else it was part of in the realm; an AI house weighs its plot before riding into a feud of its own choosing, as you do. A charter or a reckoning goes on without it, under its strongest house if it was the one leading it, or comes to nothing; a plot against the throne it led falls apart. A reckoning against a house that rides out waits for it: it neither moves nor ends while the house is away, and takes up where it left off once the house is back. If you are asked to a feud, or offer your sword, while your house is part of such a cause, you are told what you would leave behind.
- **Winning houses over.** While a feud is prepared, its claimants look for more friends; once it is declared, both sides do. Every few days the head of each side of the AI asks the house most likely to say yes. A house rides only for a cause of its own: its head is a good friend of the one who asks, or on bad terms with the other side's head, or it holds a grudge against the other house, or it is honourable and the one who asks has the better right. With none, it stays home. Silver does not buy it: once it has said yes, the head who asked pays it a compensation for keeping its men in the field (if it joined while the feud was still being prepared, once the feud begins), as one realm does when it calls an ally to a war (whatever he can spare). A house that says no is left alone for a while. Nobody is courted while the feud waits or a foe is at the walls. You may be written to with such a request, and told what you would be paid; if you head a side, you may ask the head of any house of the realm in conversation, or by an [envoy](envoys.md) (*"Ask a house to ride with us in our feud"*), and you pay the same way. A house that joins earns the other side's ill will.
- **The others remember.** A house that would not heed the crown's call with a foe at the walls, or went over to the foe, earns grievances from every other house.

A realm torn by a feud looks weaker to its neighbours, who are readier to make war on it. Open the [Factions tab](civil-war.md#the-factions-tab) to see a feud's two sides face to face.

### Three ways to press a right {#three-ways-to-press-a-right}

Your house may press a right to a fief, or a claim to a [title](titles.md#claims-to-a-title), held by another house of your realm in three ways, all under the same rules the houses of the AI follow:

- **In person:** speak to the head of the house that holds it. The line appears only when the right can be pressed now; with more than one right against the same house, you pick which after the talk.
- **By an envoy:** *"Carry your defiance to the {house}: press a right by the sword"*, from the head of that house's page in the encyclopedia (see [Envoys](envoys.md)). Your rights are listed, those that cannot be pressed greyed with the reason. When the envoy arrives it is weighed again; if it can no longer be pressed, the envoy writes to say why.
- **From the kingdom screen:** each of your rows under **Fiefs and rights** in the Factions tab has a **Press by the sword** button, greyed when it cannot be pressed, with the reason in its tooltip. It asks you to confirm, closes the kingdom screen and sends your defiance.

Each of them warns you when pressing it would mean riding against your own lord (see [Felony](#felony)), and once the feud is declared the journal says how it is won.

### What your stewards hear {#what-your-stewards-hear}

Before you choose, you are told what the AI would weigh in your place, in words and without numbers: when you confirm that you press a right by the sword (in person, by an envoy or with **Press by the sword**, whose tooltip carries only the verdict), when you bar the gates against your lord's demand, and in every window that asks you to take a side in a feud (your reckoning taking up arms, the letters of kin, friends and allies, a house courting you, your lord's call, the house attacked).

- **Who would ride**: the houses that would go with each side, a handful each, every one with *"will surely"*, *"are likely to"*, *"may"* or *"are unlikely to"*.
- **Aid from above**: whether the lord of the house attacked would send to his own lord for aid, and how likely that lord is to come.
- **The verdict**: *"Your host would outmatch theirs"*, *"You would be outnumbered"* or *"You would be evenly matched"*, and whether your host could take the walls.

Once the feud is drawn, it names the houses that do ride. It is only what your stewards hear: nothing changes for the AI.

### The lord's call {#the-lords-call}

In every feud, the head of each side calls his own **vassals**, the houses that hold their land of him; so does the lord of the house attacked when he comes to shield it, and a lord who comes to [aid](#aid-from-the-lord-above). Staying home is not one of the choices: each vassal **chooses a side**, with his lord or against him. Sellswords and the ruler's kin are not called; a vassal held prisoner chooses once he is free, if the feud is still on.

- **The vassals of the AI** ride with their lord unless they lean to the other side enough to risk their lives for it: bad blood with their lord, hatred above all, or warm ties with the other head. The fear of [felony](#felony) holds them back: more against a cruel lord, less against a merciful one, less when the other side is clearly the stronger (a calculating vassal sees it soonest), more when the lord's side is, and less for a valorous one. Even then it is never certain.
- **You:** a window, **"Your lord calls you to arms"**, tells you what is at stake and asks you to choose: **"Answer the call"** or **"Side with the {house}"**, with the warning that siding against your lord is felony.
- **Riding against one's lord** costs his regard, and he holds it against that house.
- **Counted before the defiance.** A house of the AI weighs every vassal the lords would call, on the side each would likely take, before it declares; your own choice counts once you make it.
- When a lord who is not a head rides in a feud, his vassals are likelier to ride with him, but are not called and owe him nothing for staying out.

### The lord's peace {#the-lords-peace}

A vassal never rides in two feuds at once: when his lord calls him, everything is folded into the lord's quarrel, as the old peace of the king and the Landfrieden did.

- **A vassal who only rides in another feud** (as one of its houses or an ally) leaves it, with no blame.
- **A vassal who leads a side of another feud:** that feud **pauses**. Everyone in it goes home, allies too, its clock stops and the days the fief was held are kept; its journal entry reads *(waiting for a lord's quarrel)*. When the lord's feud is over it takes up again with the time it had left, unless the houses made up or the anger went out of it by then; if it ends instead, you are told why. One not yet begun is dropped (the right remains).
- **The other head of that paused feud**, if neither lord calls him, may **keep the truce** or **ride against** the vassal in the lord's feud, on the other side; never on the same side, and never forced. A head of the AI breaks it only with a grudge against that vassal, a leaning to the other side and little honour. If it is you, a window asks: **"Keep the truce"** or **"Ride against the {house}"**.
- **You are told of everything**: a message and a line in your journal whenever a vassal of your lord, or of a head of your feuds, chooses a side, and whenever a feud of yours pauses, takes up again, is dropped or ends while it waits.

### Aid from the lord above {#aid-from-the-lord-above}

When a house is attacked, its own lord shields it, except in the rare case of bad blood between them, and then he pays for it as you would; his own lord (the duke of a count shielding his castellan, say) does not come unasked, for the vassal of my vassal is not my vassal. The lord who shields, or one who came that way, may **send a rider to his own lord for aid**, as one sends for relief of a siege; never to the crown or its kin, and not to a lord the attacker is sworn to as well.

- **An AI lord** sends for aid when the other side is the stronger. **You** get a window, **"Ask your lord for aid?"**: **"Send a rider"** (greyed with the reason when it can no longer be done) or **"We hold with what we have"**.
- **When the rider arrives,** a lord who is not free (a prisoner, or caught up in a feud of his own) cannot come, and nobody blames him. An AI lord comes more readily when he is honourable or valorous and likes the one asking better than the attacker, and never if he hates the one asking. If it is you who is asked, a window says what each answer costs.
- **A lord who comes** joins the side of the house attacked, calls his own vassals, and may in turn ask his own lord.
- **A lord who will not come** though free loses the regard of the one who asked, and if the place falls, its holder holds it against him: *"our lords did not come to defend it."*
- **Counted before the defiance**, by the chance that he comes, and only when the attackers would be the stronger without him (which is when he would be asked): a lord who would rarely come does not deter an attack, a loyal and brave one does.
- You hear of the rider, the answer, the arrival and the grudge.

### Felony {#felony}

To rise in arms against one's own lord and lose is **felony**, as in the great feudal kingdoms:

- **Who:** a house that leads a feud against its own lord (for his title, or a fief of his) and loses; a vassal that holds out against [its lord's demand](titles.md) and loses; and every vassal who, called by his lord, chose the other side, when the lord's side wins. A vassal who rose against his lord and left the feud before its end (he came home, or heeded the crown's call) still answers for it at the end if his lord's side wins; one who went to another realm is beyond his lord's reach. Nobody is judged before the feud is over.
- **The land fought over:** the house gives up to its lord what it holds in the land the feud was about, and only that: in a feud for a title, the duchy; in a feud for a fief, the lord's duchy (or county) the fief lies in. Its other towns and castles stay its own. What another house took of that land from it during the feud goes to the lord, not back to the felon. It may refuse and break with the realm, as any house that loses a fief.
- **The broken faith:** a vassal sworn to that lord also answers with his life or his purse. The lord may have him put to death: a **10%** chance, **+10%** for each level of cruelty and **−5%** for each level of Mercy of the lord, **+20%** if he rose to take the lord's title, **60%** at most. Spared, he pays his lord a fine: **30%** of his house's gold, **50,000** denars at most. The AI lord rolls; as the lord you always choose.
- **You:** as the felon, a window, **"Your lord's judgment"**, tells you what you must give up and asks you to choose; before you ride against your lord, every warning says how his temper puts your life at stake.
- **Not when** the houses made up, or the feud broke up with nobody the winner (as when [High Crown Authority](crown-laws.md#high-crown-authority) ends it). A felon still riding in another feud is judged once that one is over.
- **The AI thinks twice:** a vassal of the AI turns against his lord only when he leans far enough the other way to risk his life (see [The lord's call](#the-lords-call)), and a house of the AI declares a feud against its own lord only when its side is strong enough; both weigh the land at stake and the very odds of death above.
- You are warned before you send a defiance that would make you a felon (in person, by an envoy, from the Factions tab), and in the journal while the feud lasts.

### The crown's call to arms {#the-crowns-call-to-arms}

If an enemy besieges a town or castle of the realm while a feud is fought, the ruler calls the houses at feud to arms if the crown has **150** influence for it (if you are the ruler, you choose). With no call, the feud goes on.

- **The heads answer first**, each for its whole side, and nearly always they heed the crown: **90%**, **+10%** for each level of Honor, **−5%** for each level of Calculating (between 70% and 98%; 98% when the foe besieges a fief of their own). If a head refuses, each house of its side decides for itself, and a bitter, honourless house may, rarely, go over to the invader with its lands, if the invader is the stronger.
- **If every house heeds**, all come home and the feud waits.
- **If any house refuses**, nobody comes home yet: both sides stay in the field, still fighting each other, for **7 days of grace**. You may change your answer meanwhile: a window, **"The crown's peace"**, asks once (**"Heed the crown's call"** or **"Hold to your answer"**), and after that **"Heed the crown's call"** waits in the menu of any town, castle or village. At the head of your side, you answer for every house that rides with you. An AI house that refused thinks it over once, as the days run out (about half the time, more if it is honourable). If every house heeds before then, all come home at once.
- **When the days are out and one side heeded**, both sides lay down their arms and come home, and the feud waits. The houses that would not heed are remembered: every house of the realm outside the feud (the crown too) holds it against them, they lose its goodwill, and the ruler thinks 10 less of their head. A house that heeded when others would not wins 3 of the ruler's goodwill. Nobody is branded a traitor.
- **When neither side heeded**, the houses that heeded come home, and the crown judges each house that would not: brand it a **traitor**, at war with the realm, or **look the other way**. A ruler of the AI looks the other way more readily when the house is strong next to the crown, when he is merciful or calculating, and when he likes its head; less when he is honourable or the war goes badly. If you are the ruler, a window asks you (**"Look the other way"** or **"Brand the {house} traitors"**); if you cannot answer in time, your council decides in your name and tells you. Looked over, the house fights on in its feud with no mark, but the ruler likes its head less and holds it against it, and the houses that laid down their arms hold it against the crown. The feud goes on among those still riding in it; if one side has none left, it waits until the foe is gone; if none are left at all, it ends with nobody the winner.
- **If the foe is gone first** (at peace with the realm, or besieging nothing) before the days are out, the call lapses: nobody answers for it, and the feud goes on.
- **While a feud waits**, the grudge between its heads does not cool and there is no time limit: once the realm has been safe for a while it takes up again with the time it had left, unless the houses made up, what it was fought over is gone, or it is now beyond their reach. If lords of both sides fight side by side against the invader meanwhile, the two houses may make it up and end the feud: a **40%** chance, **+10%** for each level of Mercy and **+5%** for each level of Generosity of either head (never below 10% nor above 80%); if they do not, it goes on. A house that made it up while holding the fief keeps it. And a feud that ends with nobody the winner while the claimants already hold the fief counts as won: the fief is theirs.
- Heeding the crown does not lift a [felony](#felony): a vassal who rose against his lord still answers for it at the end.

### The host summoned for a war abroad {#the-host-summoned-for-a-war-abroad}

Just after the realm declares war on another kingdom, the crown **asks** the two heads of each feud whose hosts are in the field to set their quarrel aside and ride with the realm's host. It is a request, not a command: no days of grace, no traitors.

- Each head answers for its side. A head of the AI heeds less than one time in two: **30%**, **+10%** for each level of Honor, **−5%** for each level of Calculating, a little more the better it likes the ruler and less the more it hates the other head (between 10% and 90%).
- **Only if both heed** does the feud wait until that war is over, then take up again. If either refuses, the feud goes on; a head who refused loses **5** of the ruler's goodwill, and one who heeded loses nothing and fights on.
- At the head of a side, you get a window, **"The host is summoned against {kingdom}"**: **"Lay the quarrel aside for the war with {kingdom}"** or **"Keep to the quarrel"**. As ruler, the crown asks in your name, the same way.

**Wars and feuds.** A realm with feuds fought or paused is slower to go to war abroad: the more of its strength rides in them, the less its council (yours too) wants a new war, and less still while one is being fought; a lord may give it as his reason: *"our own houses are at each other's throats"*. While a feud runs, a realm restless after a long peace does not turn its eyes abroad; once the last one is over, it does, as if it had never waited.

## Asking a ruler to step down

A [plot against the throne](civil-war.md) that has gathered a good part of the realm asks the ruler to lay down the crown before it rises. An AI ruler only thinks it over when the plot has most of the realm behind it, far more than it needs to ask (below that he always refuses, and the plot may ask again as soon as it is strong enough), and even then a ruler with a strong claim may well refuse; a weak claim makes him likelier to agree. A ruler newly crowned is left in peace for his first year. If he does agree, the realm chooses a new ruler by its [own law](succession.md), and the plot's houses, its leader among them, vote together for the house the plot would crown, its [pretender](civil-war.md#who-it-would-crown). If he refuses, the lords hold it against him, and the plot goes on.

If you are the ruler, you are asked too, with no year of grace and from 35% of the realm's strength (not again for 90 days after you refuse). You can step down and stay as a vassal with your lands, or refuse and take what comes. Stepping down takes your whole house off the throne: none of it stands in the vote that follows (see [The crown and its claim](succession.md)).

If you lead the plot, you may ask the ruler yourself from 35% of the realm's strength, as an AI plot does; below the share at which an AI ruler would think it over, he refuses.

## Alliances between houses

Two houses of a realm may become allies, the ruler's and yours as any other.

- **By marriage.** When a head of a house, or a child or a brother or sister of one, weds someone of another house of the same realm, the two houses are allied, if their heads were on good terms before the wedding. The alliance rests on that marriage: if one of the couple dies, it holds if another couple of the two houses is still wed, or as a sworn pact if their heads are good friends; otherwise it is over.
- **By a pact.** Heads of houses who are good friends may swear to stand together, now and then, rarely. A head of the AI may write to you to offer it; you may also ask a head of house who is your good friend in conversation (*(House alliance)*), or send an [envoy](envoys.md) to ask. If he says no, give it a couple of months: ask again sooner and he only tells you his answer has not changed.
- **What it does.** Allies are called to arms in a feud (see above). In a plot against the crown, a charter or a reckoning, a house leans more to join one an ally of it leads (or, for a plot, one that would crown an ally), and far less to join one against an ally of it: the crown, or the house a reckoning is after. Allied to both, neither counts.
- **When it ends.** When a house leaves the realm (also by riding over to the rebels of a civil war), when the two end on opposite sides of a feud or one joins a reckoning against the other, when the heads of a sworn pact fall out, or when an ally refuses a call to arms.
- **Weddings.** Lords of the AI marry within their own realm far more than abroad (a wedding across realms is a quarter as likely; in the base game, half), and so do the matches offered to your house.

Your allies are listed in the [House tab](house-tab.md) of the clan screen, under *In the realm*.

## You and the factions

Talk to the head of another house of your realm:

These talks are under one line of the game's *"There is something I'd like to discuss."*: **"There are matters of the realm I would speak of, away from other ears. (Plots, factions and alliances)"**, shown only when one of them is open to you. Every line the mod adds to a lord's talk ends with a tag in brackets saying what it belongs to: *(Plot against the crown)*, *(Factions)*, *(Fief vote)*, *(Feud)*, *(Squire path)*, *(Manhunter path)*, *(Marshal)*, *(Royal court)* and so on.

- **What news from the realms of Calradia? (News of the realms)** Any lord, of any kingdom, unless he dislikes you: pick a realm and he tells you who rules it and how strong it is, its wars (with whom, for how long, who declared it and the reason its lords gave, and how it goes), its alliances, and its troubles within as far as they are known abroad: a civil war, a feud between two of its houses, a charter or a reckoning pressed in the open, lords demanding the ruler step down, talk of a plot (never who is in it), traitors being judged.
- **What is happening in the realm? (The realm's affairs)** and **Tell me of your house. (Their house)**: see [Asking how things stand](#asking-how-things-stand) below.
- **What news of the realm, my friend?** Any lord who is your friend, of any kingdom (not its ruler), tells you who is pressing whom: the factions, their aims, who stands with them, how far along they are, whether there is a darker plot against the throne, and what his own house holds against the crown. A good friend tells you more; an honourable one keeps the secrets of his own cause unless you are in it; a calculating one may remember more for a consideration. When a faction a friend is in comes to a head, he may write to you.
- **Join** a lord's charter or reckoning, or **leave** it.
- **Start a charter**, or **start a reckoning**: you choose the house, then the fief, which is for your house. (The houses of the AI may start one against your house too: you hear the rumour first, then the news when it comes out in the open.) Win other lords over one by one, in person or by an [envoy](envoys.md); how likely they are to agree is said in words.
- **Tell the ruler.** If you know of a charter still in whispers, you can take it to the ruler. He may strike at its head or keep watch, tells you which, and thanks you. Whoever told you about it will find out. A reckoning is not a plot against the crown, and is not told.
- **Ask the ruler to step down**, if you lead a plot with at least 35% of the realm's strength behind it.

When you lead a faction, you bring its laws or its claim before the council yourself, from the kingdom screen; its lords vote with you. Your factions, and those that put their case to you as ruler, have their own entry in the journal.

## Asking how things stand {#asking-how-things-stand}

Two questions sit under the game's *"There is something I'd like to discuss."*, beside the news of the realms. You can ask them of any lord of a kingdom who is not of your house, a sellsword or of a minor faction. A lord who dislikes you will not say (the same as for the news of the realms).

**"What is happening in the realm? I would know how things stand among its houses." (The realm's affairs).** The lord tells you about **his own** realm: yours, or another one if he serves another crown (then you ask *"How do things stand within {Kingdom}, among its houses?"*). He tells it page by page, the gravest first; *"Go on."* turns the page, *"That is enough for now. My thanks."* ends it. Everything he says is how things truly stand:

- **A civil war**: who raised the banner and what for (whom they would crown, a charter, or to break away with their lands), which houses rose with them, how long it has lasted, which side has the upper hand in strength and in towns and castles taken, whether the two sides may now treat for peace, and where his own house stands. A lord of the rebels tells it from their side. After a civil war, he says the realm is still healing.
- **Houses marked as traitors** and waiting for the crown's judgment.
- **Feuds** between houses of the realm, over which fief, and who rides with each side.
- **Houses that broke with the realm** and still fight it, in their leagues or on their own.
- **Charters, reckonings and a demand to step down** that are pressed in the open (never those still in whispers), with their houses.
- **Talk of a plot**: only once there is talk of one, never with names, and every lord says the same, even one who is in it.
- **Vacant titles**: a duchy (or a theme or an ulus) that the crown has yet to grant, and whether his house could hope for it.
- **Beyond the borders**: the realm's wars, and a war or a peace the council is voting on.

If nothing troubles the realm, he says so, in his own manner.

**"Tell me of your house." (Their house).** The lord tells you about his house, page by page: who leads it, its standing, its grown lords and ladies, whom it serves and what it holds; its titles, its lord and its vassals and knights (in a kingdom of [titles](titles.md)); its [rights](fief-rights.md) to the fiefs of other houses and the rights of others to its own; a claim to the throne, and by what right; its allies, friends and foes, and the grudges it bears; and where it stands with the crown: how its head gets on with the ruler, the wrong it holds against the crown if any, its oath, its offices at court, the cause it leads or is in, a traitor's mark. A house never tells you of a plot or a cause it still keeps in whispers.

## Turn it off

In the [settings file](../settings.md), set to `false`:

- `FactionsEnabled`: all of it (the plots and civil wars of before stay, alone);
- `FactionGrievancesEnabled`: the wrongs houses remember; `FactionGrievanceRelationsEnabled`: wrongs between AI houses souring their heads; `FactionGrievanceRelationsWithPlayer`: the same against your house; `FactionCrownGrievanceRelationsEnabled`: wrongs against the crown costing goodwill toward the ruler; `FactionAbandonedGrievanceEnabled`: armies that came near a besieged fief and did nothing are blamed (off: any lord of the realm near the fief when it falls spares the crown, as before); `FactionMarshalBlameEnabled`: the Marshal sharing the crown's blame; `PleaToRelieveEnabled`: the pleas of the lords of your host;
- `FactionCharterEnabled`, `FactionReckoningEnabled`, `FactionAbdicationEnabled`, `FactionConfidantsEnabled`: each part on its own;
- `FiefRightsReckoningNeedsRight` (on the [Rights to fiefs](fief-rights.md) page): a reckoning needs a right;
- `FactionFeudsEnabled`: reckonings taking up arms; `FactionFeudKingHeedsRightEnabled`: the crown respecting a firm right in a feud (off: it may forbid any feud or shield the holder); `FactionFeudDefyCouncilEnabled`: an AI reckoning taking up arms after the council's no; `FactionFeudPressRightEnabled`: a right pressed by the sword; `FactionFeudPlayerJoinEnabled`: your sword in a feud (letters, offers, hire); `FactionFeudGiveBackEnabled`: fiefs given back after a feud; `FactionStripRebelsEnabled`: a house breaking with the realm over a fief taken by a reckoning's vote; `RevokeDefectToPatronEnabled`: such a house going over to a foreign king; `RevokeFeudEnabled`: a house holding such a fief by force (it yields instead); `InternalWarWeaknessEnabled`: a realm weak in its wars being less ready for quarrels within (off: no realm counts as weak); `FactionFeudBlindEyeEnabled`: the crown looking the other way after its call to arms (off: those who refuse are branded traitors); `FeudSummonsEnabled`: the host summoned for a war abroad; `WarDuringFeudPenaltyEnabled`: feuds making a war abroad less likely; `WarWaitsForFeudsEnabled`: the restlessness of a long peace waiting while feuds run; `FelonyJusticeEnabled` (on the [Dukes, counts and castellans](titles.md) page): felony; `TitlesReliefEnabled`: asking one's own lord for aid; `FeudAllyCallEnabled`: allies called to a feud; `FeudCourtEnabled`: courting houses into a feud; `FeudAiDefiesBanEnabled`: an AI house defying the crown's ban; `FeudPartySizeEnabled`: bigger companies in a feud; `FeudStrengthCountsHiresEnabled`: what a purse could hire counted in a feud's strength;
- `HouseAlliancesEnabled`: alliances between houses; `AlliesInCausesEnabled`: allies in plots and causes; `MarriageOtherRealmEnabled`: fewer weddings across realms;
- `FactionsInAiRealms`: factions in the AI kingdoms;
- `ReckoningSmallRealmEnabled`: a small realm sparing its houses in a reckoning's vote;
- `RealmAffairsTalkEnabled`: asking a lord what is happening in the realm; `HouseTalkEnabled`: asking a lord about his house.
